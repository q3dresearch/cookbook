# Open questions

**The test:** would answering it change the decision — that new capacity means a
wait rather than an answer, and that the published approval rate is a ceiling over
a falling trend? By that test **no question is live**.

## 1. Is a withdrawal a soft refusal?

80 applications were pulled before a decision and are counted as "not granted".
Arithmetically that is right. But if applicants withdraw because a refusal is
signalled, withdrawals are refusals under another name and the real approval rate
is lower still. If they withdraw for commercial reasons — a site sold, financing
lost — they are noise and the "counting withdrawals" reading overcorrects.

**How to settle it:** compare how long a withdrawn application had been waiting
against the refusal curve. A withdrawal clustering just before the typical refusal
point is a different animal from one at three weeks. The data supports this today;
it was not done because it does not change the direction of anything.

## 2. When did the withdrawals happen?

The register records that an application was withdrawn and not when. That caps the
queue-stock chart's precision by up to 80 applications and keeps withdrawals out
of the monthly flows entirely.

**How to settle it:** nothing in this source. It would need Fiskeridirektoratet to
publish the field, or a second capture over time watching applications disappear —
which is a `wss` job, not a recipe.

## 3. Do the applicants who state a biomass figure differ from those who do not?

138 of 324 site changes state one; 85 of 86 new sea sites do. Every tonnage here
is therefore a floor drawn from a self-selected subset, and the selection rate
differs sharply by family — which is exactly the condition under which a floor
becomes misleading rather than merely conservative.

## 4. Is the slowdown load, or policy?

Approval falls and decision time rises together, which reads as a queue under
load. A deliberate tightening would more likely show refusals arriving *faster*.
But nothing here separates the two, and a rule change in 2024 would produce a
similar picture.

**How to settle it:** this is a reading problem, not a data problem —
Fiskeridirektoratet's own regulatory notices, not a scraper.

## 5. Is Norway unusual?

Scotland, Chile and the Faroes all license marine aquaculture. Whether a
50-to-60% outstanding rate on new capacity is a Norwegian phenomenon or what
marine licensing looks like everywhere would need one comparable register, and
would turn a national finding into a structural one.

## Answered

* **Why are new sites refused more?** They are not refused more so much as decided
  less: 51% of new sea site applications have never been decided at all.
* **Is the backlog concentrated in one category?** No, it is concentrated in one
  *kind* — everything creating new capacity, averaging 55% outstanding against 22%
  for modifications, with no overlap between the groups.
* **Is land-based really close to automatic?** No. That was 16 grants and 1
  refusal with 37 of 60 applications undecided. It is the most outstanding family
  in the register at 62%.
* **Has the approval rate moved?** Yes — 94% to 83% since 2024, while the median
  decision went from 71 to 200 days.
* **Is the queue clearing?** No. It has never fallen: 4 applications in 2023 to
  389 now, resolving 76% of what arrives.
* **How much capacity is held up?** 430,838 tonnes of maximum allowed biomass,
  59% of it new capacity — more than has ever been granted for new sea sites and
  new licences alike.
